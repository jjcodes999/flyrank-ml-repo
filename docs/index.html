FlyRank ML Internship, Applied Search Intelligence track (Lane 4: CTR opportunity scoring), October 2026

Which pages lose the most clicks for their Google position?

A position-adjusted review queue for search click-through problems, tested on websites it never saw

Janudi Rupasinghe Data: one month (March 2026) of the pseudonymized FlyRank internship warehouse

Repository Notebooks Metrics receipts (JSON)

Abstract

Some pages are shown on Google thousands of times but are clicked far less often than other pages at the same position, and editors need to know which of them to review first. Using one month of the pseudonymized FlyRank internship warehouse (March 2026; 75,817 pages from 37 client websites), I estimated each page's click gap from 1–15 March and checked whether its click-through rate (CTR) was still below typical for its position in 16–31 March. A transparent rule (top-20 position, at least 1,000 impressions, below-typical CTR, ranked by click gap) was compared with logistic regression and a random forest on five client-grouped folds, so every score comes from websites the method was not trained on. The rule's top 20 pages per fold were still under-performing later in 93% of cases against a 35% base rate; the random forest was slightly more precise at the very top (98%) but not deeper in the list, and its top 20 covered about half the click gap (362 vs 755 estimated missed clicks). The output is a decision-support review queue with reason codes, human checks and no-go rules for editors; it does not show that editing a page will raise its clicks.

1Introduction

In the first half of March 2026, one page in this data was shown on Google 73,639 times at an average position of 2.95. Pages at that position typically earn about 0.25% of their impressions as clicks, roughly 184 clicks for that many impressions. This page earned 18.

Gaps like this are easy to miss inside a portfolio of tens of thousands of pages, and an editor has time to look at only a few of them each week. The decision this work supports is therefore narrow and practical: which pages should a person review first for a click-through problem?

The obvious rule, "flag pages with low CTR", does not work on its own, because CTR depends heavily on position: almost nobody clicks result number 40. A fair comparison has to place each page next to pages at a similar position, and it has to account for size, because a small gap on a page seen 500,000 times matters more than a large gap on a page seen 500 times.

This paper makes three claims, each tested in the sections below:

  • C1A transparent, position-adjusted rule ranks pages whose below-typical CTR persists two weeks later at Precision@20 of 0.93 on unseen client websites, against a base rate of 0.35 (§4.1).
  • C2Learned models do not clearly beat that rule. A random forest is slightly more precise at the very top of the list, but not deeper, and it favours smaller pages carrying about half the click gap (§4.1, §4.3).
  • C3How the evaluation is split changes the conclusion: with a random split, logistic regression appears to beat the rule deeper in the list; with a client-grouped split, it does not (§4.2).

3Methodology

3.1 Unit and timeline

One row is one page. March is split at a decision moment, the morning of 16 March: everything a model may use comes from 1–15 March, and the outcome comes from 16–31 March. At the decision moment an editor could see the first half; the second half had not happened yet.

3.2 Label: is the problem still there two weeks later?

No column records whether a page was truly worth reviewing, so the label is a measured proxy. Pages are grouped into narrow position bands (0–3, 3–5, 5–7, 7–10, 10–15, 15–20, 20–30, 30–50, 50+). A page gets still_under = 1 if its CTR in 16–31 March is below the median CTR of its band in that same period. The base rate is 0.35. The label says the low CTR persisted; it does not say that fixing the page would help.

3.3 Features (all from 1–15 March)

  • impressions_h1: times the page was seen in search.
  • ctr_h1: clicks ÷ impressions × 100.
  • position_h1: average position, weighted by impressions.
  • days_visible_h1: days, of 15, with at least one impression.
  • missed_clicks_h1, the click gap: impressions × (median CTR of the page's position band − the page's CTR) ÷ 100. It is an estimate of a gap, not a forecast of clicks that could be won.

3.4 Signal checks before the rule

Before encoding a rule, I tested the two signals it leans on. Pages below the typical CTR for their position in the first half stayed below in the second half far more often (54–57%) than pages above typical (20–42%). Among below-typical pages, persistence was flat at 51% up to 1,000 impressions and then rose with volume (60%, 70%, 74%). That second check chose the rule's volume cutoff.

3.5 Baseline: a transparent rule

A page is flagged if, in 1–15 March, it was in the top 20 positions, was seen at least 1,000 times, and had a lower CTR than typical for its band. Flagged pages (8,710) are ranked by click gap; all others score 0. The rule was frozen before any model was trained.

3.6 Models

Logistic regression, with the two skewed features log-transformed and all features standardised, gives one readable weight per feature. A random forest (200 trees, minimum 20 pages per leaf, seed 42) can capture combinations of features. Both output a probability that a page stays under-performing, and pages are ranked by it. A single small decision tree was not used: it gives only a few distinct scores, so the top of the list becomes a tie broken by chance.

3.7 Validation: unseen websites only

Pages from the same website share habits (templates, brand searches, tracking), so testing on pages from websites seen in training would flatter a model. I used GroupKFold with five folds grouped by client: each method is trained on about four-fifths of the clients and scored on the remaining clients, and every client is tested once. No client appears on both sides of any fold. The rule needs no training but is scored on exactly the same folds. The folds are uneven: one fold is a single large client with 17,425 pages, and fold base rates range from 0.24 to 0.50, so methods are always compared inside the same fold. The primary metric is Precision@K (of the top K pages, the share still under-performing later), chosen because an editor works from the top of a list; AUC is reported as a whole-list check.

3.8 Leakage checks

  • Timeline. All five features come from 1–15 March and the label from 16–31 March.
  • Harness check. Adding a deliberately leaked column built from the label window raises the forest's AUC from 0.805 to 1.000, so the test setup does detect leakage. The column was then removed.
  • Train without suspects. Removing position_h1 (AUC 0.799) or missed_clicks_h1 (AUC 0.806) causes no collapse, so no single column carries the answer.
  • Population. Keeping only pages with ≥50 impressions in 16–31 March uses the outcome window to choose pages. It removed 1,723 of 77,540 pages (2.2%), and is disclosed as a limitation.
  • Head start. The rule's 1,000-impression cutoff was chosen by looking at the label on all March pages, so the rule's scores are slightly optimistic.
  • Reproducibility. Rows are sorted into a fixed order after loading; two full re-runs gave identical numbers.

4.4 What the models lean on

In the forest, shuffling position_h1 reduces accuracy the most (0.083), followed by missed_clicks_h1 (0.026); logistic regression agrees (weights −1.42 and +0.90). The role of the click gap matches the signal checks. The role of position is partly a property of the label: far down the results the typical CTR is close to 0, and a page cannot be below 0, so those pages almost always get still_under = 0. Removing position barely changes the scores (§3.8), because the click gap already carries position information. Position should not be read as a discovery.

4.5 Errors

Only 2 of the forest's 100 top-20 picks (5 folds × 20) were false alarms. Both had near-zero CTR at a top-3 position (1 click from 8,975 impressions; 4 from 5,710) and were also flagged by the rule. They looked like the clearest problems, yet were no longer below typical in the second half; the data cannot tell whether the page moved position, gained a few clicks, or had a tracking glitch. Of the pages that did stay under-performing, 5,869 were ranked in the bottom half; the largest of these were mild cases worth about 2 missed clicks each. In the rule's queue, 194 pages have an impossible average position below 1 and 1,676 had zero clicks, which led to separate data and tracking checks in the playbook (§6).

4.6 Two side findings

Pooled numbers hide differences between clients. Pooling all clients, weighted CTR by position tier follows a curve close to the one in FlyRank's March 2026 research report (Top 3 0.437%, Page 1 0.330%, Striking 0.336%, Page 3–5 0.141%, Deep 0.038%). Pooled, Page 1 and Striking look equal; for the median client, CTR drops clearly between them (0.368% to 0.245%). In the Page 3–5 tier one client supplies 73% of all impressions.

Page age does not single out CTR problems. Across five age groups, from 0–90 days to more than 365 days, 43–49% of pages were below typical CTR and 10–12% were flagged, with no clear drop-off at any age. This concerns CTR only; it does not contradict research on health scores or impressions over a page's life.

5Limitations and honest framing

  • One month. All results come from March 2026, with features and outcome each covering about two weeks. Stability across months and seasons is untested; a time-forward test on later months is the most valuable missing experiment.
  • A proxy, not proof. "Still below typical CTR" shows that a problem persisted. Nothing here shows that editing a page raises its clicks; that would need an experiment, for example editing half of the top pages and comparing them with the other half.
  • Narrow population. Only pages with search data and enough impressions in both halves: 75,817 pages from 37 clients, about 23% of March's pages. Absence from the queue is not evidence that a page is healthy.
  • Concentration. Two clients account for 72% of the rule's queue (6,266 of 8,710 pages), and one fold is a single client, so results are strongly shaped by a few websites.
  • Slightly optimistic baseline. The rule's cutoff was chosen on the same month it was evaluated on, and the population filter uses the outcome window (2.2% of pages).
  • Averaged positions. Position is an average over many searches; a page's main search could rank very differently.
  • No uncertainty intervals. Fold-to-fold variation is shown instead; small differences between methods are within it.

6Ranked recommendations: the review playbook

The rule produces the queue, because no model clearly beat it on unseen websites. Each flagged page gets one action, reason codes, and a confidence label from the forest (scored by a model that never saw the page's client).

OrderActionWhenPages
Firstcheck_data_qualityaverage position below 1; do not edit194
Secondcheck_tracking_first0 clicks in 1–15 March; confirm clicks are recorded and the page is indexed1,625
Thenreview_title_and_metaall other flagged pages, from the top of the queue6,891

Table 5. Reason codes: low_ctr_for_position on every page, plus zero_clicks, impossible_position and dominant_client (the page's client holds at least 20% of the top 100).

  1. Clear the checks, then review 20–50 pages per cycle from the top. The top 20 carry 5% of the queue's click gap at about 81 missed clicks per page; reviewing all 8,710 pages (roughly 2,900 hours at 20 minutes each) would not be worth it.
  2. Review dominant clients at site level first. When one client fills much of the top of the list, look at its templates, brand searches and search-result layout before editing pages one by one.
  3. Look at the real search result before editing: the title and description shown, any answer box or ads above the page, and whether the page matches what searchers want.
  4. Leave pages outside the queue alone. Low-CTR pages with under 1,000 impressions are monitored, not reviewed; low-CTR pages beyond position 20 have a ranking problem, not a snippet problem.
  5. Re-check monthly. Rebuild the queue and re-check the rule if the base rate moves by more than 0.10, Precision@20 falls below 0.80 on a new month, the share of zero-click pages rises above 0.25, or one client exceeds 60% of the top 50.

Never automate: rewriting or publishing titles and descriptions; deleting, merging or redirecting pages; acting on data-quality or tracking cases before the check; using the queue outside its population; judging editors or clients by page scores; or reporting the click gap as clicks that will be won.
